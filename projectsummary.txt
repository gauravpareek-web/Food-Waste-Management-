Executive summary
* Indian households generated an estimated 78.2 million tonnes of food waste in 2022—approximately 7.82 crore tonnes, or 55 kg per person per year—according to UNEP’s Food Waste Index Report 2024. India’s estimate has medium confidence, rather than being a complete national measurement.wedocs.unep
* That figure includes edible food and associated inedible material, such as peels and bones. It is not a measurement of 78.2 million tonnes of donation-ready meals thrown into dustbins.wedocs.unep
* Restaurants, caterers, hostels, and canteens are important intervention points, but the sources reviewed do not establish a sufficiently robust, separate national tonnage for Indian restaurant food waste. Do not apply global restaurant-sector percentages to India and present the result as measured Indian data. UNEP itself identifies major limitations in food-service measurement.wedocs.unep
* A defensible “number-one state for edible food waste” cannot be identified from the evidence reviewed. For municipal solid waste—not food waste specifically—Maharashtra reported the largest generation and treatment volumes in the government’s 2023–24 state dataset: approximately 24,299 and 19,877 tonnes per day, respectively.sansad
* Your platform should redistribute safe, unused food that has not been served to customers, rather than plate leftovers. This distinction follows the definition in FSSAI’s surplus-food regulations.fssai
* For five hours, build one complete workflow: donor posts → eligible NGO claims → volunteer collects → NGO confirms receipt/distribution → dashboard updates. Treat forecasting, advanced routing, and household cooked-food sharing as later phases.

Report 1: Food waste in India—scale, sources, destinations, and state comparisons
1. What exactly are we measuring?
Your problem statement combines several related but different issues.

Category	Meaning	Relevance to your platform
Harvest and post-harvest food loss	Loss during agricultural and supply-chain operations before final consumption	Important background; not the initial product focus
Household food waste	Food and associated inedible parts discarded by households	Major national problem; prevention is a useful future module
Food-service waste	Waste from restaurants, catering, institutional kitchens, and similar services	Your primary operating environment
Safe surplus food	Safe unused food that has not been served to customers	The portion your donation workflow should target
Municipal solid waste	A broader waste stream containing food waste and many non-food materials	Useful for waste-management comparisons, not food-waste rankings
UNEP’s food-waste definition includes edible and inedible fractions and multiple destinations, while FSSAI defines surplus food more narrowly for redistribution. These categories must remain separate in your report and pitch.fssai+1
Why this matters
“Food not eaten” does not necessarily mean “food that can now be donated.”
For your product, separate:
1. Safe, unserved surplus: potentially eligible for redistribution after checks.
2. Previously served food: excluded from your donation workflow.
3. Spoiled or otherwise unsafe food: excluded.
4. Inedible preparation material: not a hunger-relief resource.
The exclusion of previously served food follows FSSAI’s definition of surplus food; the regulations also prohibit unsafe-food distribution.fssai

3. How much do Indian restaurants waste?
The evidence reviewed does not support one reliable, comprehensive national restaurant-waste tonnage.
UNEP explains why food-service figures are difficult to establish:
* Studies often cover only one establishment type.
* A university-canteen study cannot automatically represent restaurants, hospitals, hotels, and event caterers.
* National estimates require broader coverage and defensible scaling.
* Some business data rely on self-reporting rather than independently verified weighing.wedocs.unep
What to put in your presentation
Use this wording:
“India has a substantial food-service measurement gap. Our platform does not only coordinate redistribution; it produces establishment-level records of surplus offered, collected, distributed, and rejected.”


That is a proposed product contribution—not a claim that your hackathon prototype has already measured India’s food waste.
Measure these four streams separately

Stream	Example	Platform handling
Preparation waste	Vegetable trimmings	Waste audit; not automatically donation eligible
Unserved surplus	Food remaining in controlled kitchen storage	Candidate for safety-screened donation
Served leftovers	Food returned on customer plates	Block donation listing
Spoilage	Deteriorated stock	Block donation; record appropriate disposal
This classification helps keep donation eligibility consistent with FSSAI’s safe, unserved-surplus definition.fssai
4. Household food waste: what your project should and should not address
The household sector is large, but a five-hour prototype should not attempt unrestricted cooked-food collection from individual homes.
For your MVP, I recommend:
* Focus operationally on restaurants, campus mess halls, and caterers.
* Keep a household education section as optional.
* Consider unopened packaged-food donations in a later, separately governed workflow.
* Do not imply that institutional donor verification automatically makes household food sharing safe.
A useful later household module could record purchase, storage, and discard reasons. However, label these as self-reported observations, not equivalent to directly weighed waste audits. UNEP excludes household recall-based surveys from its estimation approach because of measurement limitations.wedocs.unep

Solution: An Integrated Food-Surplus Prevention and Sharing Platform
The solution is to prevent unnecessary food preparation and connect safe, unserved surplus with nearby NGOs through a coordinated collection and distribution network. Its central principle is traceability: every donation should have a clear record of its source, handling requirements, collection, receipt, and final distribution.
The platform should address the complete journey—not simply display available food.
1. Prevent surplus before it is generated
Donation should operate alongside better purchasing, preparation, and portion planning.
Restaurants, caterers, and campus kitchens can record:
* Meals prepared and meals served.
* Surplus remaining after each service.
* Food discarded during preparation.
* Food returned on plates.
* Ingredients that expire before use.
* Reasons for surplus, such as cancelled bookings or lower attendance.
The platform can turn these records into establishment-level recommendations:
* Prepare food in smaller batches.
* Adjust production using attendance and booking information.
* Offer smaller portions with optional additional servings.
* Identify menu items repeatedly left uneaten.
* Improve inventory rotation and purchasing quantities.
* Establish regular surplus collection windows.
Initially, these recommendations should use transparent rules and historical patterns rather than unsupported predictions.
2. Separate donation-ready food from other waste
Not all discarded food can be redistributed. The platform should classify material before offering it for donation.

Food category	Proposed handling
Safe, unserved prepared food	Screen for redistribution
Unopened packaged food within its valid consumption period	Screen for redistribution
Food previously served to customers	Exclude from donation
Spoiled, contaminated, or otherwise unsafe food	Reject from donation
Inedible preparation material	Direct to an appropriate waste-management route
FSSAI’s surplus-food regulations define surplus as safe, unused food that has not been served to customers and prohibit the distribution of unsafe food.fssai.gov
This separation prevents the platform from treating every kilogram of kitchen waste as a potential meal.
3. Establish a verified local network
Bring together four participant groups:
* Donors: restaurants, caterers, hostels, campus mess halls, and other institutional kitchens.
* Receiving organisations: NGOs and food-distribution organisations.
* Collection partners: volunteers or designated transport teams.
* Coordinators: people responsible for onboarding, reviewing exceptions, and resolving failed collections.
Each organisation’s profile should include:
* Responsible contact person.
* Operating location and hours.
* Relevant registration or licensing information.
* Food categories handled.
* Collection and distribution capacity.
* Available storage and transport capabilities.
* Training status of relevant handlers.
FSSAI’s framework includes registration/licensing requirements, safe handling and transport, trained personnel, and record maintenance. Platform approval should document these checks rather than imply that account creation establishes compliance.fssai.gov
4. Make food listings complete and actionable
A donor should be able to create a listing quickly while supplying the information needed for a collection decision.
Required listing information
* Food name and description.
* Quantity available, preferably measured in kilograms.
* Vegetarian or non-vegetarian classification.
* Preparation date and time.
* Declared last consumption date and time.
* Confirmation that the food has not been served.
* Storage and holding information.
* Temperature reading and measurement time, where applicable.
* Packaging details.
* Pickup window and location.
* Known ingredient or allergen information, with unknown information clearly identified.
* Contact person.
FSSAI requires prepared-food labelling information including the food name, source, preparation date, last consumption date, and vegetarian/non-vegetarian classification. Its record requirements also include quantity and temperature information.fssai.gov
A photograph can help identify a listing, but it must not be treated as proof of safety.
5. Match food with the right recipient—not merely the nearest one
The platform should first establish eligibility, then rank suitable recipients.
Eligibility checks
A receiving organisation should be considered only when:
* It accepts the food category.
* It has enough capacity for the quantity.
* It can meet the handling requirements.
* Collection and distribution can finish before the declared deadline.
* The required donor and recipient checks are complete.
Matching priorities
Among eligible organisations, prioritise:
1. Feasible collection and distribution time.
2. Appropriate transport and storage.
3. Capacity to use the available quantity.
4. Proximity.
5. Demonstrated collection reliability.
Distance alone should never override a handling limitation or an infeasible deadline. The operating workflow must support distribution within shelf life and suitable handling conditions.fssai.gov
For recurring donors, scheduled collection arrangements can complement on-demand matching.
6. Coordinate collection and prevent duplicate claims
Once a receiving organisation accepts a donation, the platform should reserve it and notify the relevant participants.
The collection workflow should include:
1. NGO accepts the listing.
2. A volunteer or collection team is assigned.
3. Donor receives the expected arrival time.
4. Collector records the actual quantity and required handling observations.
5. Donor and collector confirm handover using a one-time code.
6. The receiving organisation acknowledges delivery.
Only one active claim should be allowed for the same allocation. If a collection is cancelled, the food should return to availability only after its remaining deadline and handling conditions are reviewed.
For missed pickups:
* Notify the coordinator.
* Contact an eligible backup recipient.
* Reassess whether redistribution remains feasible.
* Record expiry or rejection when it does not.
The platform should never silently extend a food’s consumption deadline to accommodate a delay.
7. Build safety into every handover
Food safety should be an operating process supported by software—not a badge generated by an algorithm.
At listing
Check required information, donor declarations, and handling requirements.
At pickup
Record quantity, packaging condition, relevant temperature information, and any reason for rejection.
At receipt
Record delivery time, quantity received, handling observations, and any discrepancy.
Before distribution
The receiving organisation follows its approved food-handling procedures and records the quantity distributed or rejected.
FSSAI requires safe handling, suitable storage and transport, and distribution within shelf life. A countdown, photograph, or donor declaration does not replace those obligations.fssai.gov
The platform should also maintain an incident workflow that allows coordinators to:
* Pause a listing or donor account.
* Notify affected participants.
* Preserve relevant records.
* Document investigation and corrective action.
8. Record what actually happened to the food
Use separate stages:
text


Listed → Accepted → Collected → Received → Distribution confirmed
Maintain exception outcomes:
text


Cancelled | Expired | Rejected | Partially distributed
Record quantities at each stage rather than carrying the original listing quantity through automatically.
For example:
* Offered: 20 kg.
* Collected: 18 kg.
* Received: 18 kg.
* Distributed: 17 kg.
* Rejected or remaining: 1 kg.
The distribution dashboard should count 17 kg, not 20 kg.
Recipient acknowledgment demonstrates reported distribution, not proof that every portion was consumed. The platform should make that distinction clear.
9. Measure outcomes without overstating impact
The dashboard should display:

Metric	What it measures
Surplus offered	Quantity donors made available
Food collected	Quantity physically handed over
Food received	Quantity acknowledged by recipients
Distribution confirmed	Quantity recipients report distributing
Rejected or remaining food	Quantity not distributed
Missed collections	Accepted donations not collected
Time to claim	Delay between listing and acceptance
Time to receipt	Duration until recipient acknowledgment
Recurring surplus	Repeated excess by item, donor, and service period
If meal equivalents are shown, disclose the assumed food weight per meal. Do not describe estimated meal equivalents as verified people fed.
Keep human redistribution separate from composting or other disposal outcomes. UNEP excludes donation for human consumption from its food-waste total, while composting remains a food-waste destination in its accounting framework.wedocs.unep
10. Provide an appropriate route for non-donatable material
Food sharing should connect to a wider waste-management system.
The platform can record referrals for:
* Appropriate organic-waste processing.
* Composting.
* Anaerobic digestion.
* Other authorised disposal arrangements.
Available routes should be verified locally rather than assumed.
Record why the material could not be donated:
* Previously served.
* Spoiled.
* Missing handling information.
* Deadline exceeded.
* No suitable recipient.
* Collection failure.
* Inedible material.
These reasons are valuable because they reveal whether improvement requires better planning, earlier listing, more collection capacity, or improved handling.
11. Address household waste through a separate prevention pathway
Households should not automatically enter the same cooked-food collection workflow as institutional kitchens.
A household-focused section can instead provide:
* Purchase and inventory lists.
* Reminders based on user-entered dates.
* Meal-planning tools.
* Portion-planning prompts.
* Self-reported discard logs.
* Information about locally approved donation options.
Any household donation service should have its own eligibility, packaging, handling, and verification procedures.
Household discard logs should be labelled self-reported. They should not be presented as equivalent to weighed waste audits; UNEP identifies limitations in recall-based household measurement.wedocs.unep
12. Create a feedback loop that reduces dependence on donation
The strongest long-term outcome is not an endlessly increasing quantity of surplus.
Provide donors with periodic reports showing:
* Which foods repeatedly remain unserved.
* Which service periods produce excess.
* How much surplus was successfully redistributed.
* How much expired before collection.
* Which planning changes reduced avoidable surplus.
Recognise participants for both successful redistribution and reduced avoidable waste. Avoid incentives based only on the number of listings or kilograms offered, which could reward overproduction.
13. Introduce the service in manageable local clusters
Start with a small, dependable network:
* A few regular institutional donors.
* A suitable receiving organisation.
* A trained collection team.
* Agreed collection windows.
* Documented safety, rejection, and incident procedures.
Expand only after the network can consistently complete collections, maintain records, and resolve exceptions.
FSSAI’s existing Save Food Share Food initiative and Indian Food Sharing Alliance provide an established ecosystem to consider when developing partnerships. The proposed platform should strengthen coordination within that ecosystem rather than duplicate it without checking local services.southregion.fssai.gov
14. Protect privacy and sustain operations
Privacy controls
* Display approximate locations before acceptance.
* Share exact pickup details only with authorised participants.
* Restrict access to contact information.
* Keep an audit trail of important changes.
* Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information about beneficiaries.
* Use organisation-level acknowledgments where individual identification is unnecessary.
Operating support
Potential funding sources include institutional service fees, CSR support, grants, and sponsored collection logistics. Track transport, packaging, training, and coordination costs separately from food-distribution outcomes.
